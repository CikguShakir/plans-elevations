7.1 · ORTHOGONAL PROJECTIONS

What an orthogonal projection is

An orthogonal projection is the image of an object on a flat plane, made by lines that leave the object and meet the plane at 90°. A line that meets a plane at 90° is called a normal to the plane.

Think of the sun directly overhead. The shadow on the ground is the orthogonal projection of the object on the horizontal plane.

Every point of the object moves straight to the plane along a normal. Nothing is tilted and nothing is drawn in perspective.

7.1 · ORTHOGONAL PROJECTIONS

What stays the same and what changes

Edge or face of the objectHow it looks in the projection
Parallel to the planeTrue length. A face keeps its true shape, size and angles.
Inclined (tilted) to the planeShorter than the true length. Angles on the face change.
Perpendicular to the planeAn edge becomes a point. A face becomes a straight line.

Example: a ramp has a sloping edge of 5 cm that rises 3 cm over a horizontal distance of 4 cm. Seen from the top, the sloping edge is drawn 4 cm long, not 5 cm.

7.2 · PLANS AND ELEVATIONS

Plan, front elevation and side elevation

  • Plan: the orthogonal projection on a horizontal plane. You look at the solid from the top.
  • Front elevation: the projection on a vertical plane, looking from the front.
  • Side elevation: the projection on a vertical plane, looking from the side.

7.2 · PLANS AND ELEVATIONS

The three types of line

Thick solid line: an edge you can see.

Dashed line: an edge that is hidden behind another part of the solid.

Thin solid line: a construction (projection) line. Leave these on your drawing.

7.2 · PLANS AND ELEVATIONS

How to draw the three views to full scale

  1. Draw the front elevation first, using the true length and height.
  2. Draw thin projection lines straight down from every corner of the front elevation.
  3. Leave a gap and draw the plan directly below, using the true width (depth) of the solid.
  4. Draw a 45° line in the corner next to the plan.
  5. Project the plan across to the 45° line, then turn and project upwards. Project the heights across from the front elevation. The side elevation sits where these lines cross.
  6. Go over visible edges with thick solid lines and hidden edges with dashed lines.
  7. Label the vertices and write the lengths.

The plan and front elevation share the length. The front and side elevations share the height. The plan and side elevation share the width, and the 45° line carries it across.

7.2 · PLANS AND ELEVATIONS

Reading the views back into a solid

You can rebuild a solid from its views because each view gives two of the three measurements.

  • Plan: length and width.
  • Front elevation: length and height.
  • Side elevation: width and height.

For a prism, the view that shows the cross-section gives the area, and another view gives how far it extends. Volume = area of cross-section × length of prism.

Example: the front elevation of the L-shaped block is an L with area 6 × 2 + 2 × 3 = 18 cm². The plan shows it is 4 cm deep. Volume = 18 × 4 = 72 cm³.

7.2 · PROBLEM SOLVING

Challenge: the skate park model

Aina makes a clay model of a skate park. The model has three parts joined together: a base (cuboid), a tower (cuboid) and a ramp (right-angled triangular prism).

Turn the 3D model and study its three views. Each square is 1 cm.

  1. Find the true length of the sloping edge of the ramp. How long is this edge when it is drawn in the plan?
  2. Find the volume of clay in the model.
cm³

Solve it with Polya's four steps

Open one step at a time. Try each step yourself before you open it.

Step 1 Understand the problem

What is given?

  • Base: 8 cm long, 6 cm wide, 2 cm high (front elevation and plan).
  • Tower: 4 cm long and 4 cm high (front elevation), 4 cm wide (plan). It stands on the base at the back left.
  • Ramp: its triangular face has a base of 4 cm and a height of 3 cm (front elevation). The ramp is 6 cm wide (plan).

What must we find? (a) The true length of the sloping edge and its length in the plan. (b) The total volume of the model.

Step 2 Devise a plan
  • Split the model into three simple solids: base, tower and ramp.
  • Read each length from a view where it is parallel to the plane, so it shows its true length. The front elevation shows the triangle of the ramp in its true shape.
  • For (a), use Pythagoras' theorem on the triangle. In the plan, a tilted edge shows only its horizontal distance.
  • For (b), use volume = area of cross-section × length for each part, then add the three volumes.
Step 3 Carry out the plan

(a) True length of the sloping edge = √(4² + 3²) = √25 = 5 cm. In the plan the edge is tilted, so it is drawn as its horizontal distance: 4 cm.

(b)

  • Base = 8 × 6 × 2 = 96 cm³
  • Tower = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 cm³
  • Ramp = ½ × 4 × 3 × 6 = 36 cm³

Total volume = 96 + 64 + 36 = 196 cm³

Step 4 Look back
  • A box that just fits around the model is 8 × 6 × 6 = 288 cm³. Our answer, 196 cm³, is smaller. That makes sense because parts of the box are empty.
  • The plan length (4 cm) is shorter than the true length (5 cm). This agrees with the rule for a tilted edge.
  • The heights agree: base 2 cm + tower 4 cm = 6 cm, the full height of the front elevation.
  • The answer is a volume, so the unit is cm³.
REVISION

Mistakes that cost marks, and key terms

  • Drawing a sloping edge with its true length in a view where it should be shorter.
  • Using a solid line for a hidden edge, or leaving the hidden edge out.
  • Forgetting an edge inside the outline, such as the top of a step.
  • Views that do not line up: the plan must sit exactly below the front elevation.
  • Rubbing out the construction lines.
